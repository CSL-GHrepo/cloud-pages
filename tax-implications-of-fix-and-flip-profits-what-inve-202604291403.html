Quick Answer: How Are Fix and Flip Profits Taxed?

Fix and flip profits are generally considered ordinary income from a business by the IRS, not long-term capital gains, meaning they're taxed at your personal income tax rate which can be significantly higher. Additionally, investors must navigate depreciation recapture and can deduct eligible expenses, all of which affect total tax owed.

“Because flipping property is often viewed as a business rather than a passive investment, the IRS taxes profits accordingly — short-term gains and ordinary income rates apply.” – Certified Public Accountant, Angela Freeman

Understanding IRS Rules for Fix and Flip

The IRS distinguishes between real estate held primarily for investment versus real estate flipped for profit as a business activity:

  • Business Income: If you buy a property, renovate it, and sell it quickly to turn a profit, the IRS treats this as business income. This income is reported on Schedule C and taxed as ordinary income.
  • No Long-Term Capital Gains: Unlike traditional rental properties held for more than a year, fix and flip profits almost always qualify as short-term capital gains or regular income. This typically subjects you to higher tax rates (up to 37%).
  • Self-Employment Tax: Because it’s a business activity, you may also owe self-employment taxes on your net profits.

According to IRS Publication 544, the intent and frequency of flipping activity play a crucial role in how income is classified.

Short-Term vs. Long-Term Gains and Depreciation Recapture

Here’s what every flipper must understand:

  • Short-Term Capital Gains: If you hold a property for less than 12 months, gains are taxed at your ordinary income rate. Fix and flip activities rarely meet the over-one-year holding requirement.
  • Long-Term Capital Gains: These rates are lower (usually 0%, 15%, or 20%), but apply only for properties held longer than a year.
  • Depreciation Recapture: If you claimed depreciation deductions on the property—as is common if you rented the property before flipping—the IRS requires you to “recapture” that depreciation and pay taxes on it at a rate up to 25% when you sell.

For example, if you rented out a house and took $20,000 in depreciation, but then decided to flip it, you must add that $20,000 back into taxable income as recaptured depreciation.

"Depreciation recapture can significantly increase tax liabilities for flippers who rent before selling—planning ahead is key to minimizing surprises." – Real Estate Tax Advisor, Mark Jacobs

Deductible Expenses in Flipping and Tax Planning

Good news: many expenses related to the fix_and_flip process can be deducted, reducing taxable income. These include:

  • Materials and supplies for repair and renovation
  • Labor costs, if you hire contractors or subcontractors
  • Interest on financing used to purchase or renovate the property
  • Property taxes and insurance during ownership
  • Advertising, utilities, and legal or professional fees

Keeping detailed records and receipts is critical. Deducting eligible expenses effectively lowers your taxable income and preserves more profit.

Strategic tax planning might include:

  • Using a separate legal entity (like an LLC) to encapsulate liability and tax benefits
  • Timing the sale to match cash flow needs
  • Consulting tax professionals to optimize deductions and credits

Can Fix and Flip Investors Use a 1031 Exchange?

Typically, the 1031 exchange—a strategy to defer taxes on investment property sales by reinvesting proceeds into a like-kind property—is not available for flip properties. The IRS expects 1031 exchanges to apply to properties held for investment or business use, not those sold quickly for profit.

However, if you have rental properties that you convert into flips, or vice versa, nuanced planning may allow limited deferment. But for the typical fix and flip investor, a 1031 exchange is rarely applicable.

She’ll report the $50,000 as ordinary business income and pay income and self-employment taxes, possibly totaling 30-40%. She deducts renovation expenses and loan interest to lower her tax burden.

Frequently Asked Questions

How are capital gains on property flips taxed compared to rental property sales?

Capital gains on property flips are usually treated as short-term gains or ordinary business income if the property was held for less than a year, leading to higher tax rates. Rental properties held longer than a year qualify for lower long-term capital gains taxes, but any depreciation claims must be recaptured upon sale.

What deductible expenses can I claim when flipping a property?

You can deduct costs directly related to acquiring, renovating, and selling the property, including materials, labor, loan interest, property taxes during ownership, insurance, utilities, and professional fees such as legal or accounting services.

Can I use a 1031 exchange to defer taxes on a fix and flip property?

Generally, no. The IRS restricts 1031 exchanges to investment or business properties not held primarily for quick resale or resale for profit, which includes most fix and flip activities. However, in rare cases where the property transitions between rental and flip use, partial deferment may be explored with professional guidance.

What is depreciation recapture, and how does it affect fix and flip investors?

Depreciation recapture is the process by which the IRS taxes the amount of depreciation previously claimed on a property when it is sold. If you claimed depreciation during rental use before flipping, you’ll need to pay tax on those deductions at a rate up to 25%, increasing your overall tax liability.
