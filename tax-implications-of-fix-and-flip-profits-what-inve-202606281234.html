Quick Overview: How Are Fix and Flip Profits Taxed?

The IRS views fix and flip profits primarily as ordinary income subject to short-term capital gains tax rates, which are typically higher than long-term rates. If you're flipping properties as a business, your income will be taxed at your marginal tax rate, and you’ll be responsible for self-employment taxes as well. Depreciation rules that apply to rental properties usually do not apply here, due to the short holding period. Additionally, while a 1031 exchange can defer capital gains on investment properties, it generally does not apply to fix and flip transactions.

Understanding Short-Term vs Long-Term Capital Gains in Fix and Flip

One of the key tax distinctions for investors revolves around how long you hold onto the property before selling:

  • Short-term capital gains: If you sell a property within one year of purchase, gains are taxed as ordinary income at your federal income tax rate, which could be as high as 37% depending on your bracket.
  • Long-term capital gains: Properties held for longer than one year may qualify for lower capital gains tax rates (0%, 15% or 20%), significantly reducing your tax burden.

Because fix and flip projects typically last just a few months, profits are almost always subject to short-term capital gains tax. According to an Investopedia analysis, this often results in investors paying higher taxes than those holding rental or investment properties long-term.

Deductible Expenses and Depreciation Rules for Fix and Flip

Unlike rental properties, fix and flip projects are treated by the IRS as business activity rather than investments eligible for depreciation. Here are key points to consider:

  • Deductible Expenses Fix and Flip: Costs directly related to the fix and flip project—such as renovation materials, contractor fees, appraisal costs, interest on loans, and marketing expenses—can be expensed and deducted in the year incurred.
  • Depreciation Rules Fix and Flip: Because properties are not held long enough to depreciate, depreciation deductions do not generally apply unless the property was rented out for an extended period before the sale, which is rare in fix and flip scenarios.

Effectively tracking all deductible expenses is critical for lowering taxable income. For example, if you spent $50,000 on renovations and $10,000 on transaction fees, these can be deducted from your gross profit, effectively reducing your taxable earnings.

IRS Guidelines Flipping Houses: Treating Fix and Flip as Business Income

The IRS treats fix and flip activity as a trade or business when the intent is to resell properties for quick profit, not as a long-term investment. Some essential IRS guidelines include:

  • Inventory vs Investment: Properties bought for resale are considered inventory, meaning profits are ordinary income, not capital gains.
  • Self-Employment Taxes: Investors involved in regular flipping must pay self-employment tax (currently 15.3%) on net profits, similar to other business owners.
  • Record-Keeping: Detailed records of expenses, purchase and sale dates, and improvement costs are essential for accurate tax reporting and audit protection.
"Fix and flip investors should understand that the IRS considers flipped homes as inventory for a business, making all profits subject to ordinary income and self-employment taxes," says John Reynolds, CPA specializing in real estate taxation.

Can You Use a 1031 Exchange for Fix and Flip Properties?

Many real estate investors are familiar with the 1031 exchange as a tax-deferral strategy on investment properties. However, it’s important to understand its limitations:

  • To qualify, the property exchanged must be held for investment or business purposes—not inventory held for resale.
  • Since fix and flip properties are sold quickly as inventory, they generally do NOT qualify for 1031 exchanges.
  • Investors should consider long-term rental property strategies if they want to leverage 1031 exchanges.

According to an article from the IRS Publication 544, 1031 exchanges apply only to property held for investment, not for resale.

Expert Tips: Proven Tax Strategies Real Estate Investors Use

Industry professionals recommend several tactics to reduce tax liability from fix and flip profits, including:

  1. Incorporate Your Business: Setting up an LLC or S-Corp can optimize self-employment taxes and potentially allow for additional tax benefits.
  2. Track Every Expense: Use specialized accounting software to capture all deductible expenses to lower taxable income.
  3. Leverage Qualified Business Income Deduction: Depending on income and business structure, investors may qualify for up to a 20% deduction on business income.
  4. Plan Project Timing: If feasible, extending the holding period beyond one year can shift gains to the more favorable long-term capital gains category.
  5. Consult a Tax Professional: Real estate tax law is complex and evolving. Experienced CPAs can help implement personalized strategies.
"Successful fix and flip investors know that tax planning is not an afterthought but an integral part of their business strategy," says Emily Park, Real Estate Tax Strategist.

Regional Considerations

While federal tax rules govern the treatment of fix and flip profits, state and local taxes vary. Some states impose additional income taxes on gains, while others have special real estate transfer taxes. It’s crucial to understand your state’s tax code to avoid surprises and optimize after-tax returns.

Frequently Asked Questions

What taxes apply to profits from a fix and flip project?

Profits from fix and flip projects are taxed as ordinary income under short-term capital gains tax rules, because the IRS considers these properties inventory held for resale. Additionally, profits are subject to self-employment tax if flipping is your business activity.

Can I use depreciation to reduce taxes on a fix and flip property?

Typically no. Depreciation is reserved for rental or investment properties held long term. Since fix and flip properties are sold quickly, depreciation deductions generally do not apply.

Is a 1031 exchange a good option for fix and flip investors?

No. A 1031 exchange defers tax on investment property gains if the property is held for investment purposes, not resale. Because fix and flip homes are inventory for resale, they usually do not qualify.

What deductible expenses can I claim on a fix and flip project?

Deductible expenses include renovation costs, contractor fees, loan interest, appraisal fees, property taxes during ownership, utilities, and marketing expenses related to selling the property. Keeping detailed records is critical for maximizing deductions.
